# Portfolio Homepage Strategy
## Dan Meier — Environmental & Experiential Systems Design

---

## 1. Strategic Positioning

### The Core Identity Statement

You are not a UX Designer with a landscape architecture background. You are a **systems designer who has spent 15 years studying how people move through, interact with, and experience designed environments** — first physical, then digital, now the convergence of both.

That framing is the entire foundation. Every word on your homepage flows from it.

**Avoid:**
- "UX/Product Designer with outdoor interests"
- Leading with job titles or software tools
- Organizing work chronologically (it implies a career pivot rather than a continuous practice)

**Favor:**
- Practice-first framing
- The word "systems" prominently and consistently
- Language that spans physical and digital environments naturally

---

### The Positioning Hierarchy

**Level 1 — What you do (practice):**
Systems design at the intersection of people, places, and experience

**Level 2 — How you think (methodology):**
Research → Frameworks → Concepts → Builds

**Level 3 — Where you operate (domains):**
Outdoor hospitality · Recreation · Public lands · Environmental experience · Digital service systems · Destination development

**Level 4 — Credentials (credibility anchors):**
MLA, The Ohio State University · 15+ years professional practice · Landscape architecture + digital systems experience

---

### Working Titles to Test

Choose one as your primary identity line. The rest can appear as descriptors.

- **Experience Systems Designer** — broadest, most transferable
- **Environmental & Experiential Systems Designer** — most precise to your direction
- **Systems Designer: People, Places & Experience** — descriptive, avoids jargon
- **Design Strategist, Human-Environment Systems** — positions toward consulting/strategy
- **[Your Name] Studio** (if/when pivoting to business entity) — leave room for this

Recommendation: **Experience Systems Designer** as the title, with the descriptor *"Designing across people, places, environments, and digital systems"* as a supporting line.

## 2. Brand Narrative Architecture

### The Narrative Spine

Your homepage needs to tell one story. Here it is in its bones:

> *Most designers specialize in one medium — screens or spaces. I've spent my career studying both, because the problems that matter most live at the seam between them: how people navigate unfamiliar environments, how operations shape experience, how places signal meaning before a word is spoken, how systems succeed or fail not in design reviews but in the real world.*
>
> *My practice is organized around understanding those systems deeply — and designing interventions that work across people, places, operations, and information simultaneously.*

This is not your About page bio. This is the **logic your portfolio is built on**. Every section of the homepage should reinforce this spine.

---

### The Differentiating Claim

What no other designer on your level can honestly say:

> "I understand a resort's wayfinding problem, a recreation app's onboarding friction, and the operational staffing system that connects them — as a single system, not three separate projects."

That integrative capacity is your moat. Name it explicitly somewhere on the homepage.

---

### Tone

- Precise, not academic
- Confident, not arrogant
- Curious, not promotional
- Direct — favor shorter sentences
- Avoid: "passionate about," "bridge the gap," "user-centric," "leverage"

## 3. Homepage Information Architecture

### Section Structure

The page should flow as a **narrative arc**, not a menu of options. Every scroll should feel like one coherent argument.

---

#### Section 1 — Identity / Hero

**Purpose:** Immediate clarity on who you are and what you do. No ambiguity.

**Elements:**
- Name
- Practice title (e.g., *Experience Systems Designer*)
- One-sentence positioning statement
- Optional: a strong visual or typographic system (no hero stock photography)
- Primary CTA: *View Work* or *Explore the Practice*

**What to avoid:** Generic "welcome to my portfolio" openers. Start mid-thought. The first line should create a small productive tension — something the reader wants resolved.

**Sample opener:**
> *"Most design problems aren't really design problems. They're systems problems wearing a design costume."*

Or more direct:
> *"I design the systems that shape how people experience places, services, and environments."*

---

#### Section 2 — The Practice

**Purpose:** Establish your intellectual framework before showing work. This is what separates a systems thinker from a project showcaser.

**Elements:**
- 2–3 sentences on your practice philosophy (systems-first, spans physical and digital)
- A brief explanation of your four-part framework: **Research · Frameworks · Concepts · Builds**
- This functions as the "operating manual" for reading your work

**Why this matters:** Visitors who understand how you think before seeing your work will evaluate it correctly. Without context, a wayfinding study looks like a UX case study. With context, it reads as environmental systems research.

---

#### Section 3 — Selected Work

**Purpose:** Proof. Organized by practice type, not by client or industry.

**Structure:**

Four work categories, each with 1–3 featured items:

| Category | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Research** | Observation, analysis, systems understanding |
| **Frameworks** | Original models, methodologies, transferable thinking |
| **Concepts** | Applied systems thinking, environmental/experiential ideas |
| **Builds** | Execution, delivery, working digital systems |

**Display approach:**
- Lead with Research and Frameworks — this is unusual and differentiating
- Avoid thumbnail grid layouts (they flatten everything into equivalent "projects")
- Consider a slightly editorial layout — let each piece breathe with a title, a short framing sentence, and a clear category label
- Do not lead with Builds, even though that's convention — it signals "I'm a producer of deliverables" rather than "I'm a systems thinker"

---

#### Section 4 — About / The Through-Line

**Purpose:** The narrative that connects landscape architecture → digital systems → convergence as a *continuous evolution*, not a career change.

**Key narrative points to hit:**

1. MLA background → trained to read environments, understand how people move through space, think about ecology and systems at scale
2. Digital systems work → translated that lens into information architecture, service design, enterprise products
3. Now → bringing both together, focused on the environments and experiences where physical and digital systems meet
4. The through-line is **systems thinking applied at different scales and mediums**

**What to avoid:** A resume timeline. The about section should read like an intellectual autobiography, not a LinkedIn summary.

**Length:** ~150–250 words. Dense enough to be substantive, short enough to be read in full.

---

#### Section 5 — Thinking / Writing (Optional but Recommended)

**Purpose:** Demonstrates active intellectual practice. Shows you generate original ideas, not just execute client briefs.

**Format options:**
- 2–3 featured essays or framework write-ups
- A "Notes" or "Field Notes" section (appropriate given your domain interests)
- Short-form observations or systems analyses (even 300-word pieces work)

**Why it matters:** In your target domains — outdoor hospitality, recreation, destination development — you will often be selling your thinking before your execution. Writing demonstrates the thinking publicly.

---

#### Section 6 — Contact / Collaboration

**Purpose:** Low-friction entry point for the right opportunities.

**Elements:**
- A brief framing of what kinds of collaborations or work you're interested in (this filters in the right people, filters out the wrong ones)
- Email or contact form
- Optional: LinkedIn, selected social
- If building toward a studio/business: a simple note like *"Available for research, strategy, and systems design engagements"*

**Sample framing:**
> *"I work with organizations trying to understand or improve complex human-environment systems — resorts, recreation operators, public land managers, destination developers, and others working at the intersection of place and experience."*

## 4. Design Direction

### Visual Identity Principles

The visual language should reinforce the intellectual content — not compete with it.

**Core principles:**

- **Systems over decoration** — Clean, structural, purposeful. No visual noise.
- **Environmental resonance** — The palette and texture should feel connected to place, land, materials — without being literal (no mountains or trees as decoration)
- **Typographic precision** — Your work is conceptually dense. Typography should be excellent. This is a signal of craft.
- **Spatial breathing room** — Whitespace (or negative space) is not emptiness. It's structure. Use it generously.

---

### Palette Direction

Avoid: tech-startup blue-gray-white, dark mode hacker aesthetic, oversaturated photography overlays.

Consider:
- Warm, muted, material-adjacent tones: stone, clay, linen, slate, sage, copper
- Monochromatic with one deliberate accent
- A palette that could work on both a digital product and a physical wayfinding system (reinforcing your dual practice)

---

### Typography Direction

- One strong serif for display/headlines (signals intellect, craft, editorial weight)
- One clean sans for body and UI elements
- No more than two typefaces
- Size hierarchy should be generous — let the biggest text be big

Possible combinations to explore:
- **Canela / Söhne** — warm serif + precise sans
- **Freight Display / GT America** — editorial + contemporary
- **Editorial New / Inter** — expressive + neutral

---

### Layout Approach

- Single-column dominant layout with intentional breakout moments
- Section headers that function as navigation anchors
- No horizontal carousels — they hide work and require effort
- Work items should be legible at a glance: title, category, one-sentence framing
- Mobile-first: your audience will often preview you on a phone

---

### What to Avoid Visually

- Stock photography of people on computers
- Case study thumbnails that look identical to every other design portfolio
- Overly technical or "designerly" micro-interactions that distract from content
- Dark-mode-only (limits accessibility and readability for longer reading)

## 5. Scalability to Business / Studio Landing Page

### The Architecture Decision

Your homepage should be built from the start as if it could become a studio site. This doesn't mean adding a "Services" page now — it means the brand language and structure don't back you into a personal-only corner.

**Decisions to make now:**

1. **URL/Domain:** Use your name (danielmeier.com or similar) as the primary, but optionally register a studio name domain and point it to the same place. When the studio identity solidifies, you can redirect cleanly.

2. **Voice:** Write in first-person singular now. Build language that can shift to "we" later without redesigning — avoid "I am a designer who..." constructions that are hard to transfer to a studio context.

3. **Practice framing:** Describe your work as a "practice" rather than "my portfolio." This language works for an individual and for a small firm.

4. **Services seed:** In your contact/collaboration section, list the *types of engagements* you take on. This is identical to a studio's services section — it just doesn't have its own nav item yet.

---

### The Studio Evolution Roadmap

**Phase 1 — Personal portfolio (now):**
- Homepage as described above
- Work organized by practice type
- About page with through-line narrative
- Contact/collaboration

**Phase 2 — Practitioner/consultant positioning (6–18 months):**
- Add a lightweight "Work With Me" or "Engagements" page
- Begin naming service areas: Research & Analysis · Framework Development · Experience Strategy · Systems Audit
- Case studies shift from "I designed X" to "The engagement delivered X"

**Phase 3 — Studio landing page (18–36 months):**
- Studio name and identity formalized
- "About" becomes "Studio" or "Practice"
- Team section (even if it's just you plus collaborators)
- Client/partner logos if appropriate
- Services and process become primary nav items

The key is that **none of these phases require a rebuild** if the architecture is right from the start.

Not everything needs to exist at launch. Here's a prioritized build order:

- [ ] A short piece of writing or field note

### Tier 3 — Add within 6 months

- [ ] Full 4-category portfolio (Research, Frameworks, Concepts, Builds)
- [ ] Expanded About / Practice page
- [ ] Writing/Thinking section with 3+ pieces

## 7. Key Strategic Decisions to Resolve

These are unresolved choices that will shape the site significantly. Decide before building.

1. **Title:** What is your primary one-line practice descriptor? (See options in Section 1)
2. **Domain:** Personal name vs. studio name, or both?
3. **Work quantity at launch:** Curate tightly (3–5 pieces) vs. launch with more? Fewer, stronger pieces > more mediocre ones.
4. **Writing presence:** Do you want writing/thinking to be visible on launch, or is it a Phase 2 addition?
5. **Case study depth:** Full narrative case studies vs. lighter "work briefs" that link to detail? (Recommendation: work briefs at launch, expand to full case studies for your best 2–3 pieces)
6. **Photography / visual identity:** Do you have work-in-progress photography, environmental documentation, research artifacts that can serve as visual assets? These would be far stronger than mock-up screenshots.

---

## 8. Positioning Test

Before finalizing any copy or structure, run it through these filters:

- Does this make a landscape architect who now works in experience strategy look coherent rather than scattered?
- Does this make a UX designer who wants to work in outdoor hospitality look qualified rather than overreaching?
- Does this make an experience systems generalist look strategic rather than unfocused?

If the answer is yes to all three, the positioning is working.

---

## 9. Immediate Next Steps

1. **Lock your practice title** — one line that answers "what do you do?" Test it by saying it out loud in a networking context.
2. **Audit your existing work** — map each project to Research / Framework / Concept / Build. Identify gaps and note which 3–5 are strong enough to lead with.
3. **Write the About narrative** — 150–250 words, the through-line. This will clarify your positioning faster than any other exercise.
4. **Draft the hero section** — name, title, one-sentence statement, and primary CTA.
5. **Choose a domain** — secure it if you haven't.
6. **Select a design direction** — palette, type, layout approach. Even a rough reference board is enough to start building.
7. **Choose a build approach** — custom code, Webflow, Framer, or similar. Given your background, Framer or custom (Next.js + Tailwind) gives you the most control and best performance signal.
